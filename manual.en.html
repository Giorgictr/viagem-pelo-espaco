Journey Through Space (Viagem pelo Espaço): manual

A 3D journey through the known universe, from your street to the edge of the observable universe, with real positions, distances and light travel times, and an adjustable speed of up to 10¹⁸ times the speed of light.

From the ground in your city to the edge of the observable universe, in a browser, with nothing to install, free and ad-free.

How to open

  1. Online: giorgictr.github.io/viagem-pelo-espaco. It works in any modern browser, including the Meta Quest one. The "My music" option only exists in the computer version.
  2. On your computer: in the Simulação 3D folder, double-click Abrir Viagem.command. It starts a small server on your own computer and opens the browser. The first time, macOS may ask for confirmation: right-click the file, choose Open and confirm.
  3. If you prefer to open it by hand, the address is http://127.0.0.1:8743. It works in Chrome, Safari and other browsers.
  4. The internet is only needed to see the Earth up close (terrain, satellite imagery and night lights), and the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Ceres, Vesta and Titan (terrain and imagery). Everything else works offline.

Getting started

  1. On your first visit, the Where to start? screen appears, with six shortcuts: land in your city, the sky on a special date (the day you were born, for example), board the ISS right now, the Pale Blue Dot with Voyager 1, fly to Saturn and Pangaea Earth. It comes back with the Home button in the "Time and display" panel.
  2. Light mode (on the same screen): turns off the 3D buildings and the physical atmosphere, uses fewer pixels and hides the belts. It turns on by itself on phones, tablets and computers with little memory, and also if the simulation is slow in the first few seconds; your choice is saved in the browser.
  3. You start near the Earth. Drag the mouse to look around and use the mouse wheel to change the speed.
  4. In the Destination panel, pick a place (the Moon, for example), check arrive in 12 s and click Go.
  5. Try the search: type "Saturn", "Betelgeuse", "Rio de Janeiro" or "Everest" and press Enter. The ship sets off for there.
  6. Press H at any time to see a summary of the controls.

Speed and trips

The speed control is logarithmic: each segment of the bar multiplies the speed, from 3.6 km/h (a walking step) up to about 10¹⁸ times the speed of light. The shortcuts take you straight to well-known speeds: on foot, car, airplane, the Parker probe (the fastest ever launched), 90% and 99% of light speed, light speed, and from 1 light-year per second up to 1 billion light-years per second.

Autopilot, in two modes

  • At the chosen speed (real time): at the speed of light, the Moon is 1.3 s away and Proxima Centauri is a true 4.2 years away. The panel shows how much is left.
  • Arrive in N seconds: the speed rises and falls by itself to arrive in the requested time, however far the destination is.

Starting point takes you straight to any body in the list. Proximity brake (in "Time and display") slows you down near planets and stars so you don't fly right past them.

Above the speed of light the trip is fiction, a "warp drive". Positions, distances and light travel times remain real.

"Where am I" panel

  • Following: the body whose motion the camera follows. Near the Earth, you follow the Earth along its orbit.
  • Closest and To the surface: the nearest body and your altitude above its ground.
  • Coordinates: latitude and longitude of the point below you.
  • From Earth, Light from Earth and From Sun: distances, and how long the light takes.
  • This light left: when the light reaching you now left the Earth, and what was happening here at that time. At the edge of the universe, this time accounts for the expansion of the universe.

Arrival cards

When you arrive somewhere (at the end of a trip or with "Go there now"), a card appears for reference and research, in parts: the body (diameter, mass, density, gravity, escape velocity, rotation, axial tilt, temperature, pressure, albedo, moons, rings, magnetic field), the orbit (mean distance, perihelion and aphelion, eccentricity, inclination, period, speed), discovery (when and by whom) and now (current distances). For stars: catalog names (Bayer, HIP, HD, HR, Gliese), coordinates, magnitude and spectral type. For exoplanets: the measured error bars and the discovery paper. At the foot of each card are the sources, with links and access date (NASA, JPL, NASA Exoplanet Archive, HYG, NED), and the Copy card button takes everything as text, ready to cite. Tap the name under "Following" to see the card again. The "Arrival cards" toggle, under "Time and display", turns them off.

On a phone, panels open one at a time: the ☰ button in the corner lists the ones that make sense right now (Speed, Destination, Earth, Where am I, Time and display, Sky, On board). The sky and on-board panels open by themselves when you enter sky mode or board a spacecraft.

Solar System

  • Planets at today's positions, calculated with the VSOP87 theory (astronomy-engine library), with errors of arcseconds over millennia. The Moon uses a full model, also with arcsecond-level error, and dynamical time (ΔT) is applied automatically. The tilt and rotation of the planets follow IAU data, and Earth's axis includes precession (the slow wobble of the axis over the centuries).
  • 21 moons, Saturn's rings with the planet's shadow, atmospheres (a halo seen from outside, a sky seen from inside), Earth's clouds, the asteroid belt, Jupiter Trojans and the Kuiper belt.
  • Time: Orbits moving / paused (T); Pace, from real time up to 10 years per second, including backward; Now returns to the current date.
  • Names (L), Orbits (O), Belts, Constellation names, Constellation lines and Radio bubble turn on and off. The constellations only make sense near the Solar System: from ~50 light-years out, the pattern fades, because the stars change places in the sky.

Radio bubble

This is the sphere reached by human radio transmissions since 1920, today about 106 light-years across, growing with the simulation date. When you leave it, a notice says that no human signal has reached that far yet.

Moon, Mars, Mercury, Ceres, Vesta and Titan up close

Below an altitude equal to the body's radius, the Moon, Mars and Mercury are drawn in tiles, like the Earth: real terrain and high-resolution imagery, loaded during flight, with more detail the closer you get. You can see the crater Tycho with its terraced walls and central peak, or descend through the canyons of Valles Marineris.

  • Terrain: the whole body with one point every ~670 m on the Moon (LRO LOLA), ~1.3 km on Mars (HRSC and MOLA) and ~940 m on Mercury (MESSENGER). In highlighted regions, one point every ~170 m on the Moon (the Apollo 11, 12, 14, 15, 16 and 17 landing sites, Chang'e 3 and 4, Tycho, Copernicus and Aristarchus) and ~330 m on Mars (Olympus Mons, center of Valles Marineris, Gale, Jezero, Gusev, Meridiani, Utopia, Elysium, Chryse and Ares Vallis; the rest of Valles Marineris at ~650 m). The credits line at the bottom shows the terrain resolution at the point where you are.
  • Imagery: NASA Trek, the Moon from the LRO wide-angle camera (~100 m per pixel), Mars from the Viking color mosaic (~330 m) and Mercury from MESSENGER (~230 m).
  • Ceres and Vesta, the two largest bodies in the asteroid belt, visited by Dawn: terrain of the whole body with a point every ~180 m on Ceres and ~100 m on Vesta, and Dawn imagery (~140 m and ~60 m per pixel). Vesta is flattened (569 × 554 × 453 km): its potato shape comes from the terrain itself, and the south pole is the 500 km Rheasilvia basin, with a 22 km central peak. With exaggerated terrain, the shape is exaggerated too.
  • Titan: the orange haze hides the ground. The Titan's haze toggle ("Time and display" panel) removes the haze and shows the surface: Cassini's near-infrared image, tinted with the color of the Huygens photo from the ground (the color is approximate), and the terrain measured by radar. Titan's terrain is gentle (−1.7 to +1.1 km) and was measured on only ~9% of the surface; the rest is interpolated (~11 km between points), with the detail of the radar strips (~1.4 km) added where they exist. Try the ×10 exaggerated terrain.
  • Pluto and Charon: New Horizons terrain (a point every ~300 m) on the hemisphere the probe saw up close in 2015, with the ice mountains around Sputnik Planitia, the "heart". The other hemisphere was only seen from afar: there the ground is smooth.
  • The moons: every moon that has been photographed shows the real images, from afar and up close. Phobos by Viking; Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto by Galileo and the Voyagers; Mimas, Enceladus, Tethys, Dione, Rhea and Iapetus by Cassini; Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, Oberon and Triton by Voyager 2; Pluto and Charon by New Horizons. What no probe photographed (the north of Uranus's moons and of Triton, the south of Pluto and Charon) appears smooth, in the surrounding average color. Black-and-white images get a little of each moon's color, and the brightness of all of them was evened out, so the colors are approximate. Phobos has its real potato shape (27 × 22 × 18 km), and Enceladus the terrain measured by Cassini. Only Deimos is still drawn: there is no global map of it.
  • Closer than that, the invented detail (toggle I) continues on top: rocks and small craters that are not in the data.
  • The photo already carries the shadows of the real terrain; lighting calculated from the terrain counts for a little less on top of it, so the shadow isn't doubled.
  • The photos were taken with a different Sun position: the shadows drawn in them do not change with the simulation time.
  • Needs internet; while the images arrive, the global texture is shown.

Exoplanets

Confirmed planets around other stars (NASA Exoplanet Archive catalog, October 2026: 6,410 of 6,445 have a known distance) are in their places. Search by planet name ("Proxima Cen b", "TRAPPIST-1 e", "51 Peg b") or star name ("TRAPPIST-1 · star with 7 planets") and go there. Stars that were not in the star catalog (most of the Kepler discoveries, faint and distant) were added to it.

  • Near a star with planets, they appear with name and orbit, and you can follow them, get close and land on the rocky ones (P). When you visit the star, you arrive from above the plane of the orbits, from where the whole system is visible.
  • Green band: the habitable zone, where an Earth-like planet could have liquid water on its surface (conservative limits from Kopparapu et al., 2013). In TRAPPIST-1, planets e, f and g fall within it.
  • What is real: size and orbital period, eccentricity and, when measured, the inclination and the argument of periastron. For planets that pass in front of their star (transit), the position along the orbit is also real, calculated from the transit times.
  • What is approximate: the orientation of the orbit in the sky (the node angle can't be measured) and, when missing, the inclination (89° for transiting planets, 60° for the rest). Without a measured radius, it is estimated from the mass (and vice versa), and the panel says so.
  • What is illustrative: the appearance. The color of each planet only indicates the type (rocky, mini-Neptune, ice giant, hot Jupiter…), based on size and equilibrium temperature. Nobody has photographed the surface of an exoplanet.
  • The "Where am I" panel shows, for the nearest planet (or the target), its size, mass, length of year, equilibrium temperature and how and when it was discovered. The equilibrium temperature is that of a body without an atmosphere: by this calculation, the Earth would be −18 °C.
  • In systems with more than 8 known planets, the 8 innermost are shown.

Landing and walking

Near a rocky planet or a moon, press P: the ship descends to eye level (1.8 m). On the ground, the arrow keys make you walk following the terrain and R takes off. Gas giants have no ground.

  • Terrain: real / exaggerated ×10 (X): shows the mountains at their true size or ten times taller.
  • Invented detail / real data only (I): up close, the ground of Mars and the moons gains procedural rocks and undulations. Turned off, only the measured data appears.
  • Level horizon (N) and Rotate with the planet (J): near the ground, the horizon stays level and you follow the rotation, like someone standing on the ground.

Stars

  • 109,000 real stars (HYG catalog), each one at its 3D position, with real brightness and color, plus 3 million Gaia stars (ESA), down to magnitude 12 (those from 11 to 12, about 1.8 million, are only downloaded once the telescope can reach them): with them, clusters and star clouds gain body, and the telescope shows many more stars. As you travel, the constellations really deform. Gaia stars can't be visited (they have no name or sphere) and are turned off in light mode.
  • The 6 stars closest to you become spheres with a granulated surface. Size and color are estimated from the star type, so you can fly right up to the surface of Betelgeuse.
  • The brightest named stars as seen from your point get a label. Click a name to pick it as your destination; double-click to go.
  • Naked-eye / enhanced sky (K): what a person would see (about 9,000 stars visible from Earth) or many more.

Milky Way and galaxies

  • Milky Way: near the Sun, the band in the sky is the real map of the whole sky made by NASA with 1.7 billion stars from Gaia, Hipparcos and Tycho (Deep Star Maps 2020): the dark Great Rift, the Sagittarius star clouds, the Magellanic Clouds. The Milky Way: real map / model toggle swaps it for the map calculated from the 3D model. Far from the Sun (hundreds of light-years), the map stops being valid and the galaxy's 3D model (bar, arms, dust) takes over. Destination: "Milky Way (seen from outside)".
  • 22 famous galaxies (Andromeda, Magellanic Clouds, Whirlpool, Sombrero…) at approximate position, distance and tilt. The shape is drawn by the program; it's not a photo.
  • 43,000 real galaxies from the 2MRS survey, out to ~2.4 billion light-years: from far away, the cosmic web appears.
  • Distant galaxies: illustrative / catalogued only (U): beyond the catalog, space can be filled with invented clusters to give a sense of scale.
  • Edge of the observable universe: an illustrative shell at ~46 billion light-years, with the cosmic microwave background pattern.

Warp and relativity

The B key switches the visual effect of speed:

  • Warp (cinema): above light speed, the stars turn into streaks, with a flash and sound when entering and leaving warp.
  • Relativity (real): what a traveler would truly see and live near light speed. The sky bunches up ahead, the stars ahead turn blue and those behind turn red, and the panel shows the γ factor ("1 s on board = 2.29 s on Earth" at 90% of light speed). The trip runs on the traveler's clock: distances contract, and at 99% of light speed the trip to Mars takes less than 2 minutes on board, while almost 13 go by outside. The simulation date moves on outside time, as in the twin paradox: coming back from Proxima Centauri, the Earth is years in the future ("Now" goes back to today). At light speed, the on-board clock stops and arrival is immediate, whatever the distance. Only light travels like that: a ship with mass never reaches c. Faster than light does not exist; it counts as light speed.
  • None.

Sound and music

The engine sound follows the speed, and there is wind near planets and inside atmospheres. Everything is synthesized on the spot. Sound on (M) and the volume control are in the speed panel. For music, there are three options:

  • Generated music: changes mood in the Solar System, among the stars and in the galaxy.
  • My music: put MP3 files (or M4A, OGG, WAV) in the musica folder. They play in random order, with a next-track button.
  • No music.

The browser only allows sound after the first click or key press.

Photo mode

Tab hides the panels and Enter saves the image as a PNG. The saved image does not include the on-screen names.

Focus mode (. or the "Focus mode" button in the "Time and display" panel): hides the panels so you travel with just the view, keeping the crosshair and the names. The ☰ button, in the corner, brings everything back. Your choice is saved for your next visit.

Probes, comets and satellites

Board real probes and comets, with the real trajectories calculated by JPL (NASA), and relive the milestone moments of each mission with real photos.

  • Probes (30):
    • outer planets: Voyager 1 and 2, Pioneer 10 and 11, Galileo, Cassini, Juno, New Horizons, JUICE, Europa Clipper;
    • Sun and inner planets: Parker Solar Probe, Solar Orbiter, Ulysses, SOHO, MESSENGER, BepiColombo;
    • asteroids and comets: Rosetta, Dawn, Stardust, Deep Impact, Hayabusa2, OSIRIS-REx, DART, Lucy, Psyche;
    • telescopes: James Webb, Gaia, Kepler, Spitzer;
    • Moon: Artemis I (the uncrewed Orion capsule, in 2022).
    Checked against official distances: Artemis I at 135 km from the Moon, MESSENGER at 202 km from Mercury, Juno at 562 km from Earth, Voyager 2 at 4,618 km from Neptune.
  • Comets: Halley, Hale-Bopp, NEOWISE, 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko and 3I/ATLAS, the interstellar visitor of 2025.
  • How to board: search for the name ("voyager 1", "halley") and press Enter; or pick it in Destination and click Board; or double-click the name in the sky.
  • On board, the mission panel shows:
    • the date and the distance from the Sun and Earth;
    • the speed relative to the Sun;
    • how long a signal takes to reach Earth;
    • a timeline of the whole mission and pace buttons (real time, 1 day, 1 month or 1 year per second);
    • and, when the mission is still active, the Now button: what the probe "sees" today.
  • Moments: a list of the highlights of each mission (for example, the Pale Blue Dot, Neptune, Pluto, "the day the Earth smiled", the Great Red Spot, the first deep field of the James Webb). When you click, the date changes, the camera turns to the target and, when there is one, the real photo appears next to the simulated scene. Moments with a photo have 📷.
  • Comets have a nucleus, a coma and two tails, which grow near the Sun: the gas tail, bluish and straight, points away from the Sun; the dust tail, yellowish, curves back along the orbit.
  • View: inside / outside: in the "On board" panel, it shows the probe in 3D, at real size, lit by the Sun, and the camera orbits around it as you drag. There are models of 20 of them (Voyager, Pioneer, Galileo, Cassini, Juno, Ulysses, MESSENGER, Dawn, Stardust, Deep Impact, OSIRIS-REx, Parker, James Webb, Kepler, Spitzer, Rosetta, Hubble, ISS…); the others, the comets and Tiangong have no model. The probe's orientation is approximate.
  • To leave, click Disembark or pick another destination.

Real-time satellites

  • The International Space Station, the Hubble Telescope and the Chinese station Tiangong are where they are right now. When you open the page, the program downloads the latest orbital elements from CelesTrak and calculates the position with the standard SGP4 model.
  • Search for "iss", "hubble" or "tiangong", or pick from Destination → Satellites (now), and board.
  • On board, the view is the one from the window: the Earth below and the horizon ahead. The camera follows the orbit, like the real station, which always keeps the same face toward the Earth (turn off Rotate with the planet to keep the camera fixed in space).
  • The panel shows the altitude (~420 km for the ISS), the speed relative to the Earth (~27,600 km/h), one lap every ~93 minutes, the point on Earth just below and whether the station is in sunlight or in the Earth's shadow.
  • Dedicated paces: real time, 1 minute, 1 hour or 1 day per second. With "1 min/s", a full lap around the Earth takes an hour and a half of simulation, or 93 seconds on screen, with 16 sunrises and sunsets a day.
  • The other satellites (selector in "Time and display"): only the ISS, Hubble and Tiangong; the ~150 brightest (default); or all active ones, about 11,000, including the Starlink trains. From space they appear as dots (dimmed in the Earth's shadow); from the ground, they only shine while still sunlit, just after sunset and before sunrise, and move from one side of the sky to the other in a few minutes. CelesTrak releases the full list only every 2 hours per address; the browser keeps the last copy.
  • From the ground, in sky mode, the ISS appears as a dot crossing the sky in a few minutes. It shines just after sunset and before sunrise, when it is still sunlit up there, and vanishes when it enters the Earth's shadow.
The scene shows what the simulation draws from that point (planets with textures, catalog stars); the photos show what the probe's camera actually captured. The moons of the other planets are at their real position between 1970 and 2040 (JPL data), which covers the flybys of Voyager, Cassini, Juno and New Horizons.

Earth up close

Below ~19,000 km of altitude, the Earth is drawn in tiles with real terrain (~30 m) and Sentinel-2 satellite imagery (~10 m), downloaded during flight. The sweet spot is flying between 1 and 10 km of altitude. On the ground the image gets blurry.

Searching and flying to a place

The same search field finds planets, moons, galaxies, named stars and places on Earth: cities (about 7,300 worldwide, only the largest), countries, seas, peaks and regions.

  • You don't need accents or the full name: "andromeda", "rio" and "everest" work, and so do nicknames such as "SP", "RJ" and "BH". Enter picks the best result (big cities win over seas and small countries); you can also click one of the suggestions.
  • The 88 constellations are also in the search ("orion", "scorpius"): the camera turns to them and the lines and names switch on.
  • For planets, galaxies and stars, the ship sets off using the mode chosen in the Destination panel. For places on Earth, it flies there from wherever you are, arrives a bit to the south and looks down at the place from above.
  • Clicking a place name on the screen does the same.

"Earth" panel

It appears at the top, in the center, when you are near the Earth. Click the title to open or close it; closed, it doesn't cover the planet. On narrow screens, it appears along with the other panels (☰ button).

  • Cities: none, large, medium or all. From high up only the largest appear; as you descend, the smaller ones show up.
  • Borders, Countries and seas, Peaks and regions.
  • Airplane mode (Y): flies following the terrain at a constant altitude, at 900 km/h. ↑ ↓ climb and descend, ← → turn.
  • Look at the sky from here (Z): see below.
  • 3D buildings (toggle, on by default): below ~6 km of altitude, OpenStreetMap buildings appear as blocks with the height recorded on the map, within a radius of a few kilometers. By day, the facades get sun and shade; at night, lit windows. Where the map has no height, the building gets two floors. They are uniformly colored blocks, not photos.
  • Daily clouds (toggle, on by default): the real clouds of each day since 2000, taken from the daily whole-planet photo made by NASA satellites (VIIRS since 2015, MODIS before). Between one day and the next, the two photos blend gradually: with time sped up, you can watch fronts and storms change. Before 2000 and on today's date (the photo comes out a day late), generic clouds or the previous day's are used.
  • Moving clouds (illustrative) (toggle): the day's clouds drift with approximate winds (westward in the tropics, eastward in the mid-latitudes). The motion is invented; the starting position is real.
  • Land in a city: type the name (the list suggests from among 7,000 cities, with state and country) and click Land there. The ship flies to the city and descends to the ground, right in its center. See the sky from there does the same and goes straight into sky mode.

At night, the city lights come from NASA's Black Marble images. On the sea, the reflection of the Sun appears.

Looking at the sky

While on Earth (anywhere, or after searching for a city), press Z or click Look at the sky from here. You stand on the ground, looking north, 35° above the horizon. The sky appears without clouds and without city lights, like a perfect night in the countryside.

  • Time and date: choose between official time (default) and solar time.
    • Official time: the time zone is determined by the nearest city (and, in Brazil, by the state), with the real history of time zones and daylight saving times that the browser knows. The panel shows the zone used ("UTC−3, Teresina time zone") and also the solar time. Far from any city in the database, the zone is estimated from the longitude, and the panel says so.
    • Solar time: the mean solar time of the place, from its longitude. It differs from the clock because of time zones (usually up to an hour, but up to 1.5–3 h in places like Spain or western China) and the "equation of time", which makes the real Sun run ahead of or behind the clock by up to 16 minutes over the year.
    The "Time and display" panel shows your computer's time, labeled as such. The Now, Tonight, 10 pm, ±1 h and ±1 month buttons help; you can also pick an exact date and time. Speed up the night makes 10 minutes pass per second and shows the sky turning.
  • By day, the bright sky hides the stars, as in reality. At twilight they gradually appear.
  • Moonlight: with the Moon above the horizon, the fuller it is, the more it brightens the sky and washes out the faint stars and the Milky Way, as on a real full-moon night.
  • You stay where you are. If you just searched for a place, you stay exactly there, not at the arrival point to the south. In sky mode, Rotate with the planet is always on, so you don't "walk" across the planet when changing the time; when you leave, it goes back to how it was.
  • The panel shows where you are looking (direction and altitude), the season in your hemisphere and which planets are above the horizon right now.
  • Names: bright stars, planets, the Moon and the constellation names, plus the compass on the horizon (N, E, S, W).
  • Constellation lines and Constellation names: two buttons in the sky panel (and two boxes in "Time and display"). Turn them on to find your bearings; turn them off to simply enjoy the sky or try to find the constellations on your own. When you enter sky mode, both start on.
  • Another city: in the sky panel, type another city and click Go. You immediately start looking at the sky from there (the local time changes according to the time zone). Useful for comparing, for example, the sky of Lisbon and of Teresina on the same night.
  • Search in sky mode: searching for a planet, a star or a constellation turns the camera toward it, instead of traveling there.
  • Deep sky (toggle in the sky panel): the 110 Messier objects and 5,288 more from the NGC and IC (nebulae, clusters, planetary nebulae and galaxies down to magnitude 14), with real size, shape and orientation. On-screen brightness follows surface brightness: large, spread-out objects, such as the Helix and Andromeda, look faint; compact ones, such as globular clusters, look vivid. They appear according to what the eye or the telescope could reach. The most famous have common names (Omega Centauri, Carina Nebula, Tarantula, Veil, Rosette, Jewel Box…); the rest appear by their number in the telescope. Search for "m13", "ngc 7000" or by name; click the name to pick it as a target.
  • Light pollution: choose between natural sky (default), very dark countryside, rural area, suburb, city and big-city center (Bortle scale 1 to 9). The more light, the fewer stars, the fewer Messier objects and the browner-orange the sky near the horizon; the Milky Way disappears from the suburb level on.
  • Refraction (on by default): lifts bodies near the horizon, as the air really does.
  • Asteroids and comets (along with the deep sky): 8,724 asteroids (from Pallas down to ones of about 15 km; Ceres and Vesta are real bodies, with terrain) and 1,991 comets, each on its own orbit, with elements from the JPL database and brightness calculated as in the official ephemerides. Checked against JPL Horizons on October 12, 2026: Ceres and Vesta at the right position to 0.01° and the right magnitude to 0.01. They appear according to what the eye or the telescope could reach; the largest asteroids and the brightest comets get a name. Search for "Vesta" or a comet's name. Limit: the orbit is calculated without the planets' pull, so very old comets or ones that passed close to Jupiter may be out of place.
  • Meteor showers (toggle in the sky panel): the main annual showers (Quadrantids, Lyrids, Eta Aquariids, Perseids, Orionids, Leonids, Geminids and others), with radiant, peak, rate and speed from the International Meteor Organization calendar, plus sporadic meteors (~5 per hour). The panel says which are active and how many meteors per hour would fit right now, depending on the radiant's altitude, the Moon and light pollution. As in real life, you need patience: 20 per hour is one every three minutes. The 10× more toggle speeds up only the meteors. The streaks are drawn at random; they are not records of real meteors.

Telescope

  • In the sky panel, turn on Telescope. The screen becomes a round eyepiece, and the panels give way to a dedicated panel in the dark margins (at the sides with the screen in landscape; top and bottom with the screen in portrait): magnification, field of view, limiting magnitude, the target with altitude and direction (and whether it is out of the field or below the horizon), the time, and the − and + buttons, Point at target, Track the stars and Exit telescope. Esc also exits.
  • Magnification: from 1× to 500×, with the bar or the mouse wheel. The panel shows the field of view (the whole Moon fits in about 30').
  • More stars: the higher the magnification, the larger the imagined aperture of the telescope and the more faint stars appear, down to magnitude ~13. Stars remain points, as in a real telescope; only planets, the Moon and galaxies show a shape.
  • Point at target: click a name in the sky (or search for an object) and use the button; the camera centers the target. The arrow keys and mouse move slowly, in proportion to the magnification.
  • Track the stars (on by default): like a telescope's motor, it compensates for the Earth's rotation and the object stays still in the center. With Telescope fixed, the sky drifts across the eyepiece: at 200×, a planet crosses the field in less than a minute.
  • Good targets: Saturn and its rings (almost edge-on in 2025–2026), Jupiter and its four moons, the phases of Venus, the craters of the Moon, Mars near opposition and the Andromeda Galaxy (an elongated smudge).

Example: the sky on a date in the past

  1. Search for the city (for example, "Teresina") and wait for the ship to arrive.
  2. Press Z.
  3. In the sky panel, pick the date (August 20, 1978) and the time (11:00 pm) and leave it on official time.

On that night, the panel shows the nearly full Moon at 41° in the east, with strong moonlight, and no planet visible to the naked eye (Neptune was above the horizon, but only with a telescope). When you leave sky mode, the simulation stays on the chosen date; use Now to return to the present.

Planets and the Moon are accurate for thousands of years backward and forward. Outside 1800–2050 the program warns you for another reason: the stars do not include their own motion (some, like Barnard's Star, move several degrees in two thousand years), and the Earth's rotation (ΔT) becomes increasingly uncertain in the distant past.

Eclipses

The Eclipses button (in the sky panel and in "Time and display") opens NASA's official calendar (Espenak and Meeus) from 1900 to 2100, starting from the simulation date.

  • View from the ground: takes you to the point of maximum eclipse, at the right date and time, in sky mode, looking at the Sun (or the Moon). In a total eclipse, it starts 1.5 minutes before totality, in real time: the sky darkens, the brightest stars and planets appear and the corona shows up around the Moon. Use the telescope to see the corona up close.
  • View from space: in a solar eclipse, it shows the Earth with the Moon's shadow passing over (1 minute per second); in a lunar one, the Moon entering the Earth's shadow and turning coppery (5 minutes per second).
  • Seen from another place: after "View from the ground", switch cities in the sky panel ("Another city" field) and click Maximum of the eclipse from here. The simulation searches for the time when the eclipse is strongest at that place and tells you how much of the Sun is covered (or how long totality lasts), setting the clock a little earlier. It also works without the calendar, for any date: it looks for an eclipse up to 4 hours before or after the simulation time. Example, on August 2, 2027: Madrid sees 87% of the Sun covered; Seville, 99%; Málaga and Luxor, a total eclipse.
  • The shadow is calculated from the real geometry of the Sun, Moon and Earth, so any date shows what would happen. The Moon's copper color comes from light passing through our atmosphere, like all the Earth's sunsets at once.
  • The calculation uses the apparent Sun (with the aberration of light), the Moon's dynamical time (ΔT) and the Earth's flattening for those on the ground, like the official calculations.
  • Accuracy: compared with the eclipse search of the astronomy-engine library, the coverage of the Sun matches to within 1% and the duration of totality is off by about 10 to 15 seconds (Luxor, August 2, 2027: 6 min 10 s versus 6 min 25 s). The Earth is drawn as a sphere; the flattening only enters the calculation for those on the ground.

Is this normal? Situations that look like bugs

Some things look like defects, but they are a natural consequence of physics or of the data, or were made that way on purpose. They are grouped by situation.

Speed and travel

What you seeWhy
"Moving now" is lower than the chosen speedThe proximity brake reduces speed near planets and stars, and the autopilot brakes on arrival ("braking"). Turn the brake off in "Time and display" if you want to fly right past.
"Stopped", but the Earth (or another planet) moves across the screenSpeed is measured relative to the body you are following. Stopped relative to the Earth, you travel along with it at ~30 km/s around the Sun.
The planets look like tiny dots or don't show up at allThe scale is real: space is almost entirely empty. Seen from Neptune, the Sun is just a very bright star. A marker helps you find each planet; to really see it, get close.
I can't enter the Sun or a starThe ship stops 2,000 km from the surface of stars (and 50 km above the cloud tops of the gas giants, which have no ground).
At the speed of light, the trip to a star takes yearsThat's the real time. Use "arrive in N seconds" or speeds in light-years per second.
The warp streaks only show up above light speedThat's the threshold chosen for the movie effect: it starts near 1 times the speed of light and becomes intense around 300 times. When you brake, the streaks vanish.

In relativity mode

What you seeWhy
The whole sky bunches up ahead and behind is nearly emptyAberration of light: near the speed of light, light from every direction seems to come from the front. It's what a traveler would truly see.
Blue stars ahead, red and dim ones behindDoppler effect: light from what you are approaching arrives compressed (bluer and stronger); light from what you leave behind arrives stretched (redder and fainter).
The Milky Way becomes an arc aheadThis is also aberration, acting on the galaxy's band.
The names pile up aheadThey follow the stars, which have bunched together. Turn off the names (L) to enjoy the view.
At light speed, the trip ends in the same instantFor someone traveling at light speed, no time passes and the distance contracts to zero: arrival is immediate, whether at the Moon or at Andromeda. Outside, the light-travel time goes by (8 minutes to the Sun, 2.5 million years to Andromeda), and the simulation date moves forward with it. Faster than light has no physics: it counts as light speed.
"1 s on board = 2.29 s on Earth" and the date running fastTime dilation: those who travel near light speed age less. The trip runs on the on-board clock, and the simulation date on the outside one; the pilot shows both ("arrival in 1.8 min on board, 12.8 min outside").
Planets shift but don't deformIt's a simplification: the center of each planet shifts like the stars, but the shape isn't distorted. In practice you barely notice, because near planets you are moving slowly.
The glare ahead isn't blindingIn real physics, the front would be blinding and much of the light would turn into invisible ultraviolet. The brightness was deliberately limited.

What you seeWhy
The constellations deform and their lines disappearThe constellations are the pattern that we see from here. As you travel, the stars change position in the sky. The lines fade out between 50 and 200 light-years from the Sun, because they stop making sense.
The star names in the sky changeThe 30 brightest named stars as seen from your point get a name. A star that is faint from here may be the brightest in the sky over there.
The Sun becomes an ordinary star, or vanishesThe Sun is an ordinary star: at ~70 light-years it's already invisible to the naked eye. Use "Enhanced sky" (K) to keep seeing it farther out.
Thousands of light-years out, the sky has few starsThe catalog of individual stars (Hipparcos) only covers the Sun's neighborhood. Farther out, the galaxy appears as star clouds, not as separate stars.

Inside the Milky Way, almost no galaxy has a nameFrom inside, only the ones visible to the naked eye get names: Andromeda, Triangulum and the Magellanic Clouds. Once you leave the Milky Way, they all appear.
"You have left the radio bubble"You passed the point to which human transmissions have reached (~106 light-years).
At the edge of the universe, the light takes "13.8 billion years", not 46The universe expanded while the light was traveling. Today's distance (~46 billion light-years) is greater than the travel time. Beyond ~100 million light-years, "Light from Earth" starts to show how long the light from here takes to reach the Earth; the light leaving the Earth today would never get that far.
At the edge, half of the sky is a colored patchIt's the illustrative representation of the cosmic microwave background, the limit of what can be observed.

System limits

  • Data accuracy:
    • star distances reliable out to a few hundred light-years;
    • terrain of the Moon, Mars and Mercury with 0.7–1.3 km of detail (170–330 m in highlighted regions);
    • Earth with ~30 m terrain, ~10 m imagery and ~600 m night lights;
    • ancient Earth at ~110 km.
  • Internet: required for the Earth up close. If a tile server fails, the program tries again, but a patch may take a while to become sharp.
  • What doesn't exist (yet):
    • 3D buildings;
    • the real hour-by-hour motion of clouds (only each day's photo);
    • eclipse shadows (the Moon's shadow on the Earth, Jupiter's on its moons);
    • shadow of the rings on Saturn;
    • exoplanets;
    • artificial satellites other than the ISS, Hubble and Tiangong;
    • the exact position of the moons of the other planets before 1970 and after 2040;
    • the Sagittarius Dwarf Galaxy as it is: in reality it is invisible to the naked eye, and here it appears only as a very faint smudge.
  • Simplifications:
    • relativity shifts and colors, but doesn't deform, the planets;
    • the physical atmosphere calculates single scattering per light ray and approximates the rest (deep twilight is estimated). Only the Earth and Mars get this calculation; Venus, Titan and the giants use the simple atmosphere. There are no rainbows, ice halos or real clouds;
    • refraction uses the standard atmosphere; on very cold or hot nights, the real one changes by a few arcminutes;
    • the size and color of stars up close are estimates.
  • Illustrations:
    • the shape of the 22 famous galaxies;
    • galaxies beyond ~1 billion light-years (toggle U);
    • the edge of the universe;
    • the invented ground detail (toggle I);
    • the colors of ancient Earth;
    • the glacial ice.
  • Performance: weaker computers may slow down when flying low and fast over the Earth or with everything on. See "Common problems".
  • Phone and tablet: they work with touch buttons, but the experience is designed for keyboard and mouse.

Common problems

What happensWhat to do
The page doesn't openThe server isn't running: open it through Abrir Viagem.command.
In Safari, "localhost" doesn't openUse http://127.0.0.1:8743 (the launcher already uses this address).
A change doesn't show upReload once with Cmd + Shift + R.
The Earth up close is blurry for a few secondsThe tiles are arriving over the internet. If a server drops the connection, the program tries again by itself.
No soundClick once on the screen (the browser requires a gesture) and check that the button says Sound on.
The screen turns white and shows "the graphics card ran out of memory"It happens after a long time flying low over the Earth, with thousands of tiles. The simulation reloads by itself and returns to the same place, date and epoch. If it happens again in under a minute, close heavy tabs and reload by hand.
SlowTurn off Belts and Orbits, use "Naked-eye sky" and avoid flying very low and very fast over the Earth.
Slow only near the ground of the Earth or MarsThe Physical atmosphere does a calculation for every point on the screen. Switch to Atmosphere: simple.
